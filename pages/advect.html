Advect

Generative wallpapers for macOS. Thousands of particles march through a layered noise field and leave glowing filaments behind them on a near-black ground. Every render is unique, or pass a seed and get the same picture back.

Get the app

Download Advect.dmg ↗ — drag it to Applications and it lives in your menu bar.

Requires macOS 13 or later. Universal, so it runs natively on Apple Silicon and Intel. Signed and notarized, so it opens with a double click. Nothing else to install: the renderer is compiled into the app, which is 567 KB.

From the menu bar you get:

  • New Wallpaper — render at your display's native resolution and set it
  • New Day Cycle — a Dynamic .heic whose colour drifts with the time of day
  • Auto-Rotate — a fresh wallpaper every 30 minutes through once a day
  • Recent — thumbnails of past renders, click to re-apply, ⌥-click to reveal
  • Palette, size and frame-count pickers, and Launch at Login

While it paints, the wallpaper itself redraws about ten times a second, so you watch the picture appear at full size on the desktop. Cancelling puts your old wallpaper back. Wallpapers are set through NSWorkspace, so the app never asks for Automation permission — it needs no permissions at all.

The day cycle

A Dynamic wallpaper packs 32 frames that share one seed, so the geometry is frozen and only colour and light drift. The result changes continuously through the day rather than snapping between looks: harbor red at night, ember orange at dawn and dusk, dune gold through the morning and midday, moss purple in the afternoon.

The command line

advect is a standalone Python renderer with no dependencies at all — standard library only, writing real PNGs and macOS Dynamic .heic files by hand.

advect                    # render to ~/Pictures/advect/, print path
advect --set              # render AND set as desktop wallpaper
advect --palette ember    # harbor | ember | moss | dune
advect --seed 7 --size 2016x1310
advect --dynamic --set    # a Dynamic .heic that shifts with the time of day

It compiles a small C hot loop on demand with cc, and falls back to a pure-Python march when there's no compiler. Same geometry either way.

How it works

A particle is dropped at a random point and repeatedly asks the noise field which way to go, leaving a tapered, anti-aliased streak as it travels. Some strokes are painted sharp, straight into the image; the rest deposit energy into a coarse light grid that gets blurred and added back as glow. That split is why the filaments read as lit rather than merely drawn.

The app doesn't shell out to the Python — the renderer is ported to Swift, with the same C march linked in at build time. Keeping the two honest means reproducing CPython's Mersenne Twister exactly, down to init_by_array seeding and genrand_res53, because a seed has to name the same picture from either one. Output is verified pixel-identical across seeds, sizes, every palette, and both architectures.
